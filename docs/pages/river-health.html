Prototype 2 of 5 · River health

What we're proposing

Turn the Benefits Report into a permanent page that grows each year: the four things the tunnel was built to improve, before and after; a step-through of what happens when it rains; a page for each overflow connection; a short monthly update; and a plain account of how the figures are counted.

Why

The Benefits Report is the best evidence Tideway has, and today it exists as a PDF linked twice from one news story. The homepage counter is updated monthly but nobody can see what happened in a given month, and its only explanation is a 13px footnote. Overflow data is also becoming something the public expects to check for themselves, and Thames Water's live map already shows it for the wider network. A page that holds the results and links to that map puts Tideway's evidence where the conversation is.

Headline figures are from the Benefits Report (12 Oct 2026). Per-overflow and monthly figures are sample data until Tideway shares the real ones.

The four objectives, before and after

1 · Sewage discharges

About 86% less overflowing into the tidal Thames

Before21m m³
2025<3m m³

Annual storm overflow volume fell to just under three million cubic metres, against an average of more than 21 million before Tideway. 2025 was unusually dry, but the report finds the fall is far larger than rainfall alone would explain.

2 · Water quality

Improved water quality in the tidal river

Chart: dissolved oxygen by stretch, before and after [figures from report]

The report measures improvement in water quality across the tidal river. Its findings sit alongside ZSL's State of the Thames report, which also found signs of recovery.

3 · Sewage litter

84 tonnes of sewage litter kept out of the river

Before30%
202518%

Wet wipes, sanitary products and plastics. Around 130 tonnes were caught across the wider tunnel system. On the foreshore, Thames21 found sewage-related items fell from 30% of litter recorded to 18%.

4 · Health risk for river users

Far fewer days with an elevated health risk

Before75.7%
202510.4%

Days with an elevated health risk linked to storm overflows fell from an average of 75.7% of days to 10.4% in 2025. That matters to rowers, paddlers and anyone on the foreshore.

Step 1 of 5

Rain falls on London

London's sewers, built in the 1860s, carry rainwater and sewage in the same pipes.

The sewers fill up

In heavy rain they fill quickly. They were built for a city of about four million people.

Before the tunnel: overflow into the river

To stop sewage backing up into streets and homes, overflows released the excess into the Thames.

Now: down into the tunnel

The overflows are connected to the 25km tunnel, 7.2m across, falling from 31m below Acton to 66m at Abbey Mills. It can hold 1.6 million cubic metres.

On to Beckton for treatment

The Lee Tunnel carries it to Beckton Sewage Treatment Works, where it's cleaned before returning to the river.

How we count this

The total volume of combined sewage and rainwater diverted into the London Tideway Tunnel system instead of spilling into the river. One tonne is about one cubic metre. It's updated once a month.

Level and flow monitors at each connection record how much enters the tunnel. [Method to confirm with Tideway.]

Discharges from overflows that aren't connected to the tunnel, and discharges further upstream on the non-tidal river.

The amount captured follows the rain. A wet month means more captured; a dry month, less. That's why the Benefits Report compares against what rainfall alone would predict.
